# Acceptance criteria — Provenance Guard

Five criteria that say what "working" means for this service, written in unit 7
**before** the full evaluation was run. The targets below were committed before
any evaluation results existed and are not edited after seeing results (see the
unit 8 note at the bottom for how a revision would be recorded instead).

> Drafted with AI assistance (Claude) and left for my own review — see
> "How I Used AI" in the README.

Two data sets are used below. Both are fixed files in `eval_data/`, chosen
before scoring anything:

- **Known-human set** — 20 passages of 60–200 words taken from Project Gutenberg
  books published before 1930, so no language model could have written them.
  At least 6 of the 20 are deliberately *plain* writing (children's readers and
  simple first-person narrative), because plain writing is the case signal one
  is most likely to accuse.
- **Known-AI set** — 10 passages of 60–200 words generated by Claude for this
  project and labelled as model-generated in the file. They are never used as
  "human" anywhere.

Scores are deterministic for a fixed input (gpt2 runs in eval mode, no
sampling), but each criterion is still measured on **three trials** and must
hold on **all three**.

---

## 1. False positives on known-human writing

**Measurement:** Send each of the 20 known-human passages to the live
`POST /submit` (real gpt2 detector) and count how many come back with
`guess == "ai"` (the high-confidence AI label).

**Target:** At most **1 of 20** (5%) per trial, on every one of three trials.

**Why this target:** On a writing site a wrong accusation costs the writer far
more than a missed detection costs the site, and the people most likely to be
accused (plain, second-language, young writers) have the least room to argue
back. One in twenty is already more than I'd like; anything looser describes a
system nobody should ship.

**How verified:** `python evaluate.py` (criterion 1 block), which records every
response to `results/`.

**Pass / fail:** PASS if the count is ≤ 1 in all three trials; FAIL otherwise.

---

## 2. Score separation between known-AI and known-human text

**Measurement:** On the same live runs, the mean `confidence` (combined score,
0 = human, 1 = AI) of the 10 known-AI passages minus the mean combined score of
the 20 known-human passages.

**Target:** Gap of at least **0.20** on every one of three trials.

**Why this target:** A conservative threshold only makes sense if the two
populations actually sit apart. If the gap is under 0.20 the unsure band
(which is 0.40 wide) swallows nearly everything and the labels carry no
information. 0.20 is half the unsure band — wide enough that the score means
something, not so wide that it assumes a detector better than gpt2 can be.

**How verified:** `python evaluate.py` (criterion 2 block).

**Pass / fail:** PASS if the gap is ≥ 0.20 in all three trials; FAIL otherwise.

---

## 3. Every label is reachable, and the ranges have no gaps or overlaps

**Measurement:** (a) For every score in a 0.0001-step sweep over [0, 1], plus
the exact thresholds and ±0.0001 either side, `score_to_label` returns exactly
one of the three labels and the labels change only at the configured
thresholds. (b) Across the 30 live submissions of one trial, count which of
the three labels appear at least once.

**Target:** (a) **0** gaps and **0** overlaps; (b) **all 3** labels appear at
least once in each of three trials.

**Why this target:** A label that no real text can reach is a label that was
designed but never tested, and a gap between ranges means some score gets no
label or the wrong one. Requiring real submissions (not just a unit test on
numbers) proves the thresholds are reachable by the signals as they actually
behave.

**How verified:** `python -m pytest tests/` (threshold tests) for (a);
`python evaluate.py` (criterion 3 block) for (b).

**Pass / fail:** PASS if both (a) and (b) hold in all three trials; FAIL
otherwise.

---

## 4. The appeal path always works

**Measurement:** Against the live service: submit 5 texts, appeal each
returned `content_id` once, then appeal 3 made-up `content_id`s. Check the HTTP
status, the returned `status`, and the audit log.

**Target:** **5 of 5** valid appeals return `200` with `status ==
"under_review"` and add exactly one `appeal` entry to `logs/audit.jsonl`
carrying the appeal reason; **3 of 3** unknown ids return `404` and add no
appeal entry. Every trial.

**Why this target:** The appeal is the only thing between a wrong accusation
and nothing at all. "Usually works" is not acceptable for it — a single lost
appeal is a writer who was accused and then ignored.

**How verified:** `python evaluate.py` (criterion 4 block) and the appeal tests
in `tests/`.

**Pass / fail:** PASS only at 100% on both counts in all three trials; FAIL
otherwise.

---

## 5. Bad input is refused cleanly, never crashes

**Measurement:** Send a fixed set of 12 malformed requests to the live
`POST /submit`: non-JSON body, wrong content type, a JSON list instead of an
object, missing `text`, empty `text`, whitespace-only `text`, non-string
`text`, missing `creator_id`, empty `creator_id`, non-string `creator_id`,
`text` over the size limit, and `text` too short to score.

**Target:** **12 of 12** return a `4xx` status (never `5xx`) with a JSON body
containing an `error` field, and **0** of them produce a decision entry in the
audit log. Every trial.

**Why this target:** A crash on malformed input is one of the most common real
failures there is, and a 500 tells the caller nothing about what to fix. Bad
input is fully under my control, so anything short of 100% is a bug, not a
trade-off.

**How verified:** `python evaluate.py` (criterion 5 block) and the validation
tests in `tests/`.

**Pass / fail:** PASS only at 12 of 12 with 0 decision entries in all three
trials; FAIL otherwise.
